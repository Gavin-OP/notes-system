# Rules of Thumb

## Learning Objectives
- Recognize and understand common financial abbreviations used in the industry.

- Differentiate between various types of stocks and their characteristics.
- Explain fundamental concepts in derivatives, including futures and options.

## Introduction
Welcome to "Rules of Thumb" – your practical guide to the essential language of finance. This lesson is designed to be a foundational reference, a quick-access glossary that will help you navigate complex financial discussions and documents with confidence.

### Financial Abbreviations
In the fast-paced world of finance, time is money, and efficiency is key. That's why many commonly used terms are shortened into abbreviations. Understanding these acronyms is crucial for reading financial news, reports, and even for everyday conversations in the financial world. They act as shorthand, allowing professionals to communicate complex ideas quickly.

Here are some of the most frequently encountered abbreviations you'll come across:

| Abbreviation | Full Term | Brief Explanation |
| :----------- | :-------- | :---------------- |
| **APR**      | Annual Percentage Rate | The annual rate charged for borrowing or earned by an investment, including interest and other fees. |
| **WACC**     | Weighted Average Cost of Capital | The average rate a company expects to pay to finance its assets, considering the proportion of each component of capital (e.g., equity, debt). |
| **NPV**      | Net Present Value | The difference between the present value of cash inflows and the present value of cash outflows over a period of time, used to evaluate investment profitability. |
| **ROI**      | Return on Investment | A performance measure used to evaluate the efficiency of an investment or compare the efficiency of several different investments. |
| **EBITDA**   | Earnings Before Interest, Taxes, Depreciation, and Amortization | A measure of a company's operating performance, often used as a proxy for cash flow. |
| **IPO**      | Initial Public Offering | The first time a private company offers its stock to the general public, becoming a publicly traded company. |
| **STIR**     | Short-Term Interest Rate | Refers to interest rates on financial instruments with short maturities, typically less than one year. |
| **IMM**      | International Monetary Market | A division of the Chicago Mercantile Exchange (CME) where currency and interest rate futures are traded. Often associated with the "IMM Index" for Eurodollar futures, which expresses interest rates as 100 minus the annual interest rate. |

These abbreviations are more than just shortcuts; they are gateways to understanding complex financial concepts. For instance, when you see "APR," you immediately know it refers to the total cost of borrowing, including interest and other charges, expressed as an annual rate, without needing to spell out the entire phrase.

### Key Finance Dictionary Terms

With a grasp of common abbreviations, let's now expand our vocabulary to include fundamental financial terms. We'll group these terms by related areas to help you build a cohesive and practical understanding.

#### Understanding Risk and Insurance
Risk is an inherent part of finance, from personal investments to corporate strategies. Insurance is a primary tool for managing and mitigating these risks. Understanding the language of risk and insurance is crucial for protecting assets and making informed decisions.

*   **Peril**: At its core, a peril is simply the **cause of a loss**.
    *   **Example**: If your house burns down, **fire** is the peril. If your car is stolen, **theft** is the peril.
*   **Indemnification**: This fundamental principle in insurance means that after a loss, the insured party is **restored to their approximate financial position prior to the occurrence of the loss**. The goal is to make you "whole" again, not to allow you to profit from the loss.
    *   **Example**: If your car is damaged in an accident, the insurance payout should cover the repair costs to bring it back to its pre-damage value, not give you extra money.
*   **Insurable Risk**: Not all risks can be insured. For a risk to be considered insurable, it generally needs to meet several requirements:
    *   There must be a large number of similar exposure units (e.g., many houses, many cars) to predict average losses.
    *   The loss must be accidental and unintentional.
    *   The loss must be determinable and measurable (you can prove it happened and quantify its value).
    *   The chance of loss must be calculable to set an adequate premium.
    *   The premium must be economically feasible for people to afford.
    *   There should be no catastrophic loss that affects too many insureds at once (e.g., a widespread natural disaster that bankrupts the insurer).
    *   **Example**: Most personal, property, and liability risks (like car accidents or house fires) are insurable because they meet these criteria.
*   **Non-insurable Risk**: These are risks that are difficult or impossible to insure, often because they don't meet the criteria for insurable risk.
    *   **Example**: Market risks (like a stock market crash), financial risks, production risks, and political risks are typically non-insurable by traditional insurance policies.
*   **Reinsurance**: This is essentially "insurance for insurance companies." An insurance company purchases insurance from another insurer (the reinsurer) to reduce its own risk exposure. This helps spread the risk among many insurers and provides access to more financial resources, especially for large or catastrophic claims.

*   **Hazards**: These are conditions that increase the chance or severity of a loss.
    *   **Physical Hazard**: Physical conditions that increase the chance of loss.
        *   **Example**: Icy roads increasing the chance of a car accident, or defective wiring increasing the chance of a fire.
    *   **Moral Hazard**: Dishonesty or character defects that increase the chance of loss. This often involves intentional actions.
        *   **Example**: Faking an accident to collect insurance money, or intentionally inflating a claim amount.
    *   **Attitudinal Hazard / Morale Hazard**: Carelessness or indifference to a loss, which increases its frequency or severity. This is often unintentional.
        *   **Example**: Leaving your car keys in an unlocked car, making it easier to steal, or not bothering to fix a leaky roof because "insurance will cover it."
    *   **Legal Hazard**: Characteristics of the legal system or regulatory environment that increase the chance of loss.
        *   **Example**: Large damage awards in liability lawsuits, making insurers more exposed to higher payouts.

#### Understanding Stocks and Dividends
When you invest in stocks, you're buying a piece of a company. Understanding the different types of stocks and how companies distribute profits is fundamental to equity investing.

*   **Stock**: A share of stock represents a claim on the company's assets and earnings, signifying ownership.
    *   **Common Stock**:
        *   Represents a **residual claim** on the firm's cash flows, meaning common shareholders get paid after everyone else (creditors, preferred shareholders) in the event of liquidation.
        *   Receive dividends at management's discretion (not guaranteed).
        *   Typically have **voting rights** to elect a board of directors, giving them a say in company management.
    *   **Preferred Stock**:
        *   Receive **fixed dividend payments** before common stockholders. These dividends are often a set percentage of the par value.
        *   Dividends can be skipped, but any skipped dividends must usually be paid in full in the future (cumulative preferred stock) before common shareholders receive anything.
        *   Usually have **no voting rights**.
*   **Cyclical Stocks**:
    *   Businesses are highly **sensitive to economic trends**. Their performance tends to follow the overall economic cycle, performing well during expansions and poorly during recessions.
    *   Stock prices are often very volatile.
    *   **Example**: Companies in industries like automobiles, travel and leisure, banks, furniture, and high-end clothing retailers. When the economy is booming, people buy new cars and take vacations; when it slows, these purchases are often delayed.
*   **Defensive Stocks**:
    *   Businesses are relatively **stable over business cycles**. They tend to perform consistently regardless of economic ups and downs because they provide essential goods and services.
    *   Cash flow is very stable, and they often pay high dividends, making them attractive to income-focused investors.
    *   **Example**: Companies in sectors like utilities, food, beverages, drugs, and insurance. People still need electricity, food, and medicine even during a recession.
*   **Dividend**: A payment made by a corporation to its shareholders, usually as a distribution of profits.
    *   **Mechanism**: When a company announces a dividend, it sets several key dates that determine who receives the payment:
        *   **Declaration Date**: The date the board of directors officially announces the dividend, its amount, and the associated dates.
        *   **Ex-Dividend Date**: The first day a stock trades without the right to receive the declared dividend. To receive the dividend, an investor must purchase the stock *before* this date. On the ex-dividend date, the stock price typically falls by roughly the amount of the dividend, reflecting that new buyers will not receive the upcoming payment.
        *   **Record Date**: The date on which a company determines which shareholders are eligible to receive the dividend. You must be a shareholder of record on this date (typically one business day *after* the ex-dividend date to account for trade settlement).
        *   **Payment Date**: The date when the dividend is actually paid to eligible shareholders.

#### Understanding Derivatives: Futures and Options
Derivatives are financial contracts whose value is *derived* from an underlying asset, such as a stock, commodity, or currency. They are powerful tools for hedging risk or speculating on future price movements.

*   **Futures**: A futures contract is a standardized legal agreement to buy or sell an asset at a predetermined price on a specified future date. Both parties are *obligated* to fulfill the contract.
    *   **Price Limits**: The maximum amount that a futures price can move up or down during a single trading session. This helps prevent extreme volatility and allows market participants to react to news.
    *   **Circuit Breakers**: If a price limit is reached, trading may temporarily stop to allow the market to cool down and reassess.
    *   **Position Limits**: The maximum number of contracts a speculator may hold, designed to prevent excessive speculation and market manipulation by any single entity.
    *   **Margin**: To trade futures, investors must deposit funds into a margin account as a good-faith deposit.
        *   **Initial Margin**: The amount required to open a futures position.
        *   **Maintenance Margin**: A minimum balance that must be maintained in the margin account. If the account falls below this level due to adverse price movements, a **margin call** is issued, requiring the investor to deposit additional funds.
        *   **Variation Margin**: Funds deposited to bring the margin account back up to the initial margin level after a margin call.
        *   **Marking to Market**: The daily adjustment of the margin account balance based on the settlement price of the futures contract. This ensures that gains and losses are settled daily, preventing large accumulated debts.
*   **STIR (Short-Term Interest Rates)**: Refers to interest rates on financial instruments with short maturities, typically less than one year. These are often the underlying asset for interest rate futures contracts.
*   **IMM Index (International Monetary Market Index)**: An alternative way to express interest rates, particularly useful for futures contracts traded on the IMM. It's defined as `100 - R`, where `R` is the interest rate expressed as a percentage per annum. For example, if the interest rate is 3% p.a., the IMM index is 97.

*   **Option**: Unlike futures, an option is a contract that gives the holder (buyer) the **right, but not the obligation**, to perform a particular transaction (buy or sell an underlying asset) on or before a specific date (expiration date) at a specified price (strike price). The seller of the option, however, *is obliged* to fulfill the contract if the holder chooses to exercise it.
    *   **Writing an Option**: This simply means selling an option. The writer receives a premium from the buyer.
    *   **Exercising an Option**: Invoking the right granted by the option contract.
    *   **Call Option**: Gives the holder the right to **buy** the underlying asset at the strike price. Buyers of calls expect the price to rise.
    *   **Put Option**: Gives the holder the right to **sell** the underlying asset at the strike price. Buyers of puts expect the price to fall.
    *   **American Option**: Can be exercised at any date before (and including) the expiration date.
    *   **European Option**: Can only be exercised on the expiration date.
    *   **In the Money (ITM)**: An option is ITM if exercising it immediately would result in a profit.
        *   Call Option: Underlying asset price (S_t) > Strike Price (K)
        *   Put Option: Underlying asset price (S_t) < Strike Price (K)
    *   **At the Money (ATM)**: An option is ATM if the underlying asset price is equal to the strike price (S_t = K).
    *   **Out of the Money (OTM)**: An option is OTM if exercising it immediately would result in a loss.
        *   Call Option: Underlying asset price (S_t) < Strike Price (K)
        *   Put Option: Underlying asset price (S_t) > Strike Price (K)
*   **The Greeks**: In options trading, "The Greeks" refer to a set of Greek letters (Delta, Gamma, Theta, Vega, Rho) that measure the sensitivity of an option's price to various factors. Each Greek letter stands for the sensitivity of an option to a particular type of risk, such as changes in the underlying asset's price (Delta), volatility (Vega), or time to expiration (Theta).

#### Understanding Bonds: Duration
Bonds are debt instruments, representing a loan made by an investor to a borrower (typically corporate or governmental). Duration is a critical concept for understanding a bond's risk, particularly its sensitivity to interest rate changes.

*   **Duration**: Duration is a measure of a bond's price sensitivity to changes in interest rates. In simpler terms, it tells you how much a bond's price is likely to change for a given change in interest rates. It also provides an estimate of how quickly an investor can recover their initial investment, acting as a kind of "effective maturity." A bond with a higher duration is more sensitive to interest rate changes, meaning its price will fluctuate more significantly when interest rates move.
