# Interview Review

An interview is a two-way street for learning. Right after it ends, the questions, follow-ups, and your feelings are still fresh. Capture these details quickly to turn the experience into a stronger foundation for your next conversation. Reviewing isn't about self-judgment or re-litigating every word; it's about finding patterns and making small, actionable adjustments.

## Record Facts First, Then Feelings

Separate objective observations from subjective interpretations. This makes your review more helpful and less prone to endless speculation.

You can note down:

- Interview round, format, duration, and participants.
- Actual questions asked and the main points of your answers.
- Interviewer's follow-up questions and areas where they showed particular interest.
- Moments you got stuck, misunderstood something, or wished you'd added more.
- New observations about the role's content, team dynamics, or company culture.
- The next steps and timeline explained by the interviewer.

For example, "They asked about data definitions twice" is a fact. "They definitely didn't like me" is an inference. Keeping them separate makes your review more productive and less prone to endless speculation.

## Four Layers of Your Answer

Evaluate your responses across these four dimensions. Some answers might have good content but are too long; others might be well-structured but lack personal detail. Identifying which layer needs work helps you focus your preparation for next time.

1. **Did it answer the question?** Did you circle around without addressing the core?
2. **Was the structure clear?** Were the conclusion, actions, and results easy to follow?
3. **Was the evidence specific?** Did you explain your contribution, judgment, and outcomes?
4. **Was the connection authentic?** Did your motivation and understanding of the role genuinely align with this company?

## Distinguish Three Types of Signals

Not every weak answer means a fixed ability gap. Look for recurring patterns across multiple interviews to decide where to invest your preparation time. A single technical question you couldn't answer doesn't define your capability. But if the same gap appears repeatedly across several target roles, it's worth adding to your LeetCode, domain knowledge, or project practice.

- **Quick adjustments:** Answers that were too convoluted, irrelevant examples, or overlooked company research.
- **Ongoing development:** Repeated gaps in specific professional knowledge or skills.
- **Fit information:** Your genuine feelings about the work content, team interaction, and types of challenges presented.

## Handling Follow-Up & Thank You

A thank-you email can be brief and impactful: express gratitude for their time, mention one specific point of discussion, and reaffirm your continued interest. If you made a factual error or promised to provide additional materials, concisely correct or deliver them. There's no need to write a lengthy apology for ordinary expressions of regret.

After a rejection, a polite request for feedback is reasonable, though no reply is also common. Lack of feedback doesn't mean the interview failed for one identifiable reason; many factors outside your control (like headcount, internal candidates, or timing) also play a role.

## Treat the Interview as Your Own Research

Recall how interviewers described daily work, challenges, new hire support, and success metrics. Which answers made you more excited, and which made you hesitate? The interview process can't represent everything after joining, but it offers real insight for your offer decision.

## Look for Patterns Across Multiple Interviews

When you review several interviews together, you'll often see patterns: which types of roles you advance further in, which stories are most persuasive, which questions repeatedly trip you up, and what kind of team environment feels most natural to you. A single outcome is influenced by many factors; *repeated patterns* are what truly inform a change in your approach.

## AI Assistance for Review

AI tools can help organize your interview notes or test revised answers for clarity and structure. For example, paste a question and your answer, then ask AI to identify areas that lack specificity or could be more concise. Remember, the final reflection, personal examples, and genuine insights should always come from you. Use AI to assist your critical thinking and self-assessment, not replace it.
