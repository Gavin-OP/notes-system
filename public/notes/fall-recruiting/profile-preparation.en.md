# Preparing Your Resume and Profile

Your application materials are like different facets of the same person: your resume quickly highlights your suitability, LinkedIn showcases your professional experience and skills, and makes it easy for recruiters, alumni, and peers to learn more about you and connect. A Cover Letter adds context to your motivation, while a portfolio or personal website can house projects and experiences that don't fit on your resume.

At this stage, you can first build a complete set of materials, then gradually adjust them for specific roles. How to write your resume, LinkedIn profile, and other materials will be covered in separate notes later.

## First, Build a Unified Content Library

You can start by putting your education, projects, internships, extracurriculars, and skills into a content library. For each experience, you can record the background, your actions, the results, relevant data and quantifiable achievements, as well as your reflections at the time. When you need to prepare application materials, you can then select from this library.

Your content library can be much more detailed than your final resume. For example, for a single internship, you can record the projects you worked on, the tools you used, the people you collaborated with, the problems you encountered, and the final outcomes. When applying for different roles, you can then select the most relevant parts.

This not only saves time but also ensures consistency of basic facts across different versions.

## Decide Which Materials You Need

- **Resume:** The most common core material in the hiring process, and an essential preparation.
- **LinkedIn:** Ideal for showcasing professional experience and skills, researching target companies and teams, and building professional connections with recruiters, alumni, or peers.
- **Cover Letter:** Prepare this only when a role explicitly requires it, or when you need to explain your motivation and suitability for the job.
- **Portfolio:** Use this when you have multiple works or project case studies that need to be systematically presented.
- **Personal Website:** Use this when you need to centrally present your personal introduction, experiences, and long-term projects.

More materials aren't always better; choose based on the role requirements and your own situation.

## Be Consistent, But Don't Fully Repeat

The company names, dates, job titles, educational experiences, and key results across all your materials should align, but the emphasis can change depending on the purpose of each material.

For example, for the same experience:

- Your resume can highlight the actions and results most relevant to the target role.
- LinkedIn can provide more professional background and context.
- Your Cover Letter can explain why this experience influenced your career choices.
- A portfolio can further showcase the specific process, methods, and visual outcomes.

Recruiters should see the same person from different entry points, while also gaining new information.

## Keep Multiple Versions for Different Roles

Your application materials will usually change with your target roles. You can keep a complete Master Version and then generate different versions based on the specific roles.

Between versions, you can adjust the order of experiences, emphasis, keywords, and project selection, while continuing to draw from the same content library.

As new internships, projects, and skills are added, you can also regularly update your content library. This way, when you enter the actual application phase, your main task becomes selecting and adjusting existing evidence, rather than organizing your experiences from scratch each time.
