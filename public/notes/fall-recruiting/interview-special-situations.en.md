# Handling Unusual Interview Situations

Interviews don't always go as planned. Sometimes the interviewer is late, a link breaks, the format changes unexpectedly, or you encounter an inappropriate question. Knowing your options beforehand can really help you stay calm and make good decisions in the moment.

## Interviewer Lateness or No-Show

First, double-check the interview time, time zone, meeting link, and any recent emails for updates. If the interviewer is late, it's usually a good idea to wait a reasonable interval. Then, send a polite, neutral message to your recruiter or primary contact, letting them know you're ready and asking if there's been a change in timing. Avoid assigning blame; there might be a genuine emergency.

If you have another commitment that creates a hard stop, professionally state your availability and suggest rescheduling. Record the incident as part of your overall impression of the process, but remember that unexpected delays can happen to anyone.

## Technical Glitches

Technology can be unpredictable! Before your interview, it's a good idea to test the actual platform, camera, microphone, and internet connection. Have a charged phone and your interviewer's contact details (phone and email) ready as a backup.

If a technical issue occurs during the interview:
1.  **Briefly state what happened:** "It seems my connection dropped for a moment."
2.  **Attempt to rejoin or switch channels:** Try re-entering the meeting, switching to a different device, or using a phone for audio if video fails.
3.  **Communicate:** If you can't restore the connection quickly, send a quick email or text to your contact explaining the issue and asking for a backup link or to reschedule.
4.  **Continue professionally:** Once resolved, pick up where you left off. If the disruption significantly prevents a fair interview, it's totally reasonable to request a new slot rather than struggling through.

## Last-Minute Format Changes

Sometimes an interview format changes without much notice – a one-on-one becomes a panel, an online meeting shifts to in-person, or an unexpected test is added. It's perfectly normal to ask for clarification.

You can politely ask your recruiter or contact about:
*   The new format and its purpose.
*   Who will be attending.
*   The expected duration.
*   Any specific preparation or materials needed.

If the change affects your accessibility needs, travel arrangements, study schedule, or care responsibilities, discuss an adjustment with the recruiter. They often have flexibility to ensure a fair process for all candidates.

## Inappropriate Questions or Requests

You might encounter questions that feel too personal, discriminatory, or irrelevant to the job. Your goal is to keep the conversation focused on your qualifications and the role's requirements.

**Examples of sensitive topics:** Family plans, marital status, health, age, appearance, religion, political views, or requests for confidential information from previous roles.

**How to respond:**
*   **Clarify relevance:** "Could you help me understand how that question relates to the essential requirements of this role?"
*   **Redirect:** "I prefer to keep the discussion focused on my professional qualifications and how I can contribute to this position."
*   **Decline private information:** If a question touches on a legitimate work requirement (e.g., travel), you can confirm your ability to meet that requirement without disclosing personal details: "I can confirm that I am able to meet the travel requirements for this role."
*   **Politely decline:** "I'm not comfortable discussing that topic during an interview."

If the interaction continues to cross professional boundaries, you have the right to pause or end the interview. Protecting your boundaries is a sign of professionalism.

**Confidential Information or Unpaid Work:**
Definitely never share confidential information from previous employers, clients, or academic projects. Similarly, avoid submitting fabricated work. Instead, focus on your personal contributions, methods, and the anonymized context of your experience. Be wary of requests for extensive unpaid "trial work" or personal financial details, which can be red flags for scams.

## Suspected Scams

Unfortunately, job scams exist. Keep an eye out for these red flags:
*   Requests for personal financial information (bank account, credit card, social security number) early in the process.
*   Offers that seem too good to be true, or pressure to accept immediately.
*   Requests for money for "training," "equipment," or "visa processing."
*   Communication from generic email addresses or messaging apps rather than official company domains.
*   An interview process that feels rushed, unprofessional, or lacks clear details.

If you suspect a scam, stop communicating immediately, do not share any personal information, and verify the company and role through official channels (like their official website or LinkedIn).

## Emergency Rescheduling (Your Side)

Life happens! If you need to reschedule an interview due to an unexpected emergency (e.g., sudden illness, family emergency), communicate promptly and professionally with your recruiter or contact person. Explain briefly that an unforeseen circumstance requires you to reschedule, apologize for any inconvenience, and offer a few alternative times or ask for their availability.

## Factual Documentation and Calm Follow-Up

After any unusual or difficult interview situation, it's helpful to:
*   **Document:** Record the facts, including dates, times, participants, and the exact wording of any problematic questions or incidents. Distinguish clearly between facts and your feelings about them.
*   **Reflect:** Consider what the situation reveals about the organization's culture or process. An interview is a two-way street; you're also assessing them.
*   **Follow-up:** If appropriate, send a concise thank-you note. If you need to provide feedback on an incident or decide not to proceed, do so professionally.
*   **Seek Guidance:** If you experienced discrimination or harassment, it's wise to consult relevant local official equality or employment guidance. For example, in Hong Kong, the [Equal Opportunities Commission](https://www.eoc.org.hk/en/enquiries-and-complaints/complaints-handling-procedures) provides guidance on discrimination complaints.
