# Understanding Roles and the Market

When you first start looking at job postings, the sheer variety of role titles can easily feel overwhelming. Building a high-level map first can make it much easier to understand: how industries operate, where companies fit in, what roles exist in the market, and how these roles relate to each other.

You can narrow your focus step-by-step, following "Industry → Company → Role Market → Role Essence → Career Path," before diving into specific job searches and applications.

## First, Understand How Industries Create Value

The same major can lead to many different industries, and the same industry requires completely different functions. When researching an industry, you can start by looking at:

- Who it solves problems for and where its revenue comes from;
- Who the upstream, downstream, customers, partners, and regulators are;
- How technology, policies, consumer habits, or economic cycles are changing it;
- Which parts are expanding and which skills are in increasing demand.

Trying to explain how the industry operates in your own words is often enough to help you understand why companies within it exist and why certain roles are needed.

## Next, See Where Companies Fit in the Industry

After understanding the industry, you can further observe which part of the value chain specific companies are responsible for.

If you have enough time and energy, you can pay attention to the main products, customers, regions, competitive strategies, recent priorities, and organizational structures of leading companies in the industry or your target companies. Financial reports, official websites, product pages, job postings, and public interviews can offer different perspectives; experience-sharing posts on social media can also supplement your understanding with real-world insights.

Company size, stage of development, and business model can also change the day-to-day experience of the same role. In larger, more established companies, organizational divisions might be clearer, and training and processes more complete; in smaller teams, role boundaries might be more flexible, and individuals may get to touch more aspects of the work.

Therefore, even if the job title is the same, it might mean different work content and learning opportunities at different companies.

## See What Roles Actually Exist in the Market

After understanding the industry and companies, the next step is to see what kind of people these organizations need to get work done.

Only knowing about one popular role can easily lead to putting all your preparation into a narrow path. By first understanding the families of roles, team divisions, and interrelationships within an industry, you'll find it easier to discover entry points that suit you.

"Quant" is a typical example. "Wanting to be a Quant" might encompass Quantitative Research, Quantitative Trading, Quantitative Development, risk modeling, model validation, and roles related to data and strategy. The titles and divisions aren't entirely consistent across different companies, and the emphasis on math, programming, market knowledge, and communication skills will also vary.

## Understand the Essence of the Work, Not Just the Job Title

Some titles that look different might actually involve similar tasks. For example, a Data Analyst, Business Intelligence Analyst, and some Business Analysts might share data extraction, metric analysis, and communication/presentation tasks; product, operations, and project roles might also have significant overlap in requirements analysis, collaboration, and driving initiatives.

## Finally, Look at Career Paths and Transferable Skills

After understanding the work content of a role, take one more step forward: if you enter this role, where might it lead you?

You can pay attention to:

- What industry knowledge, tools, and judgment methods you can learn;
- What common next-step promotions and development directions are;
- Which skills would still be useful if you change companies or industries in the future;
- Whether new hires can receive feedback, training, and opportunities to take on complete tasks.

The value of a job comes not only from its title but also from the kind of skill capital it allows you to accumulate.

Career paths are rarely perfectly straight lines. By understanding transferable skills, you'll have more room for choices and can reduce the pressure of feeling like "your first job determines your entire life."
