# How to Land Your First Internship

When you haven't yet experienced a real work environment, how do you choose your first role? And with no work experience on your resume, how do you find your first internship? Perhaps these are the questions you're grappling with.

Don't worry. Everyone's career journey starts from zero, and your first internship isn't meant to begin with "already having lots of experience."

## Preparation: When Building Your Profile and Interviewing, Use Evidence Instead of "I Don't Have Experience"

The hardest part about looking for your first internship is not having existing work experience to prove yourself, which can easily make you feel like there's "nothing to write" on your resume. At this point, you might consider: besides jobs and internships, have you done anything related to the skills required for the role?

Course projects, student organizations, part-time jobs, volunteer activities, and personal projects can all be starting points. The key is to clearly explain:

*   What problem you were facing;
*   What role you played;
*   How you moved the project forward;
*   What results you achieved.

For example:

*   Data analysis projects, research projects, or competition experiences from your courses can serve as evidence when applying for data analysis roles;
*   Campus projects, startup competitions, student organization activities, or product solutions you designed can demonstrate product thinking and project management skills;
*   Social media operations, campus event promotion, or student organization publicity can align with marketing, content, and growth-related roles;
*   Coursework, Hackathons, GitHub projects, or small tools you've built can help software development roles understand your technical abilities.

These experiences don't need to be large-scale. A small project that clearly explains the problem, actions, and results is often more convincing than an experience with just a title and no specific content.

Once you've decided which roles you'd like to apply for, you can read a few JDs for similar positions to identify recurring requirements, then look back at your own experiences for evidence. For instance, data analysis roles might frequently mention SQL, Excel, Python, Dashboard, Business Analysis, and Communication.

Instead of just writing:

> Knows Python.

You can elaborate by explaining:

> Used Python to clean and analyze public data, and completed data visualization.

Instead of just writing:

> Good communication skills.

You can elaborate by explaining:

> Collaborated with team members on a market research project, responsible for coordinating task assignments and presenting the final results to the professor.

When recruiters see specific actions, it's easier for them to understand what skills you already possess.

## Choosing: Prioritize Opportunities That Allow Newcomers to Engage in Real Work

When looking for your first internship, you might consider broadening your definition of "internship." Beyond formal programs at large companies, you can also look into university labs, startup teams, non-profit organizations, projects with professors or alumni, and short-term opportunities with clear timeframes.

A significant part of the value of your first internship comes from whether you can engage with real problems and gradually understand how a job is done in a real-world setting. A two-month internship at a startup, if it allows you to genuinely participate in user research, data analysis, or product design, might offer more learning value than an internship at a well-known company where your main responsibility is organizing files.

When reviewing roles, you might focus on these pieces of information:

*   Does the JD clearly state what specific tasks you will be involved in;
*   Will you be able to engage with real problems the team is solving;
*   Will someone be able to provide basic guidance or feedback;
*   Will you have the opportunity to complete a relatively self-contained piece of work;
*   And finally, can you produce deliverables, results, or an experience you can talk about.

One of the goals of your first internship is to start accumulating this evidence that can be understood by your next recruiter.

## Applying: You Can Also Proactively Create Opportunities for Your First Internship

If formal recruitment channels aren't yielding results for now, you can also proactively seek out smaller, more accessible real-world projects.

For example, reach out to:

*   Professors and research teams;
*   Alumni startups;
*   University entrepreneurship programs;
*   Student organizations;
*   Non-profit organizations;
*   Hackathon teams;
*   People around you who are working on projects.

The job titles for these opportunities might not be standard, but if they allow you to genuinely solve problems, they can still become valuable experiences for your future job search.

For instance, helping a student organization organize event data can also develop into a concrete project experience:

> Analyzed event registration data from previous years, identified key participant demographics, and adjusted promotional channels for the next semester based on the analysis.

What's truly valuable here is what work you completed, what judgments you made, and how the results were used.

## During the Internship: Let Your First Internship Leave "Assets" for Your Next Experience

After a good internship concludes, it can usually leave you with several things for your future career development, such as:

*   A new tool skill;
*   A complete project experience;
*   A set of data or results that can be included in your resume;
*   A colleague who can provide a recommendation or feedback in the future;
*   A STAR Story that can be used for interviews;
*   A more specific understanding of a particular role or industry.

These "assets" don't necessarily appear automatically at the end of an internship. Actively observing, documenting, and organizing during your internship can make the same few months generate more long-term value.

### Observe How Full-Time Employees Work

As an intern, the tasks you encounter are often just one part of the overall workflow. Beyond completing your own tasks, you can also pay attention to how full-time employees move their work forward:

*   What steps a task goes through from proposal to completion;
*   How the team determines task priorities;
*   Who people consult and what resources they check when information is insufficient;
*   How different teams communicate and divide responsibilities;
*   What issues managers focus on in meetings;
*   What the standards are for a mature analysis, PRD, code, or presentation;
*   What the differences are in how Senior and Junior employees approach problems.

Many professional insights that are hard to come by in a classroom are hidden within these everyday work practices.

If conditions allow, you can also understand the upstream and downstream aspects of your tasks. When responsible for a Dashboard, you might ask who will use it and what decisions the data ultimately supports; when developing a feature, you can learn where the requirements come from and how its effectiveness will be judged after launch. This makes it easier to build a holistic understanding of the role, rather than just remembering "what tasks I completed."

### Document Your Work

After a few months of interning, it's easy to forget the specifics of what you did. When it's time to prepare your resume and interviews again, you might only remember "I worked on a project" but struggle to recall the details.

### Actively Understand "Why This Way"

During an internship, it's easy to focus entirely on "how to complete tasks." If you want to gain more from an experience, you can also ask an additional layer of questions:

> Why does this task exist?

For example:

*   Why did the team choose this metric?
*   Why was this feature prioritized for development?
*   Why is this problem most important to the client?
*   Why did the Senior use this analysis method?
*   Why wasn't the other solution adopted in the end?

Understanding these contexts can help you gradually move from executing tasks to understanding the decision-making logic behind the work.

### Organize Disparate Tasks into Reusable Stories

Before your internship ends, you might review a few of your most representative projects. See if you can answer:

*   What was the background and objective at the time?
*   What were my specific responsibilities?
*   What actions did I take, and why did I do them this way?
*   What challenges did I encounter?
*   What results were produced in the end?
*   If I were to do it again, how would I adjust?

This content can later be transformed into Resume Bullets, Portfolio pieces, STAR Stories for behavioral interviews, and project descriptions for your next interview.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### 1. Should my first internship be for exploration, or to pave the way for my next experience?

Your first internship often serves two purposes simultaneously: helping you confirm what you enjoy, and adding more compelling evidence for your next application. Which one you prioritize can depend on how certain you are about your career direction.

If you don't have a clear direction yet, your first internship can serve more as a career exploration function. You might observe:

*   What problems these roles primarily solve on a daily basis;
*   What skills are used in the work;
*   Do you prefer independent analysis or frequent communication;
*   Do you prefer long-term product development or quickly switching between different projects;
*   Does your interest in the role come from the work itself, or from the job title and external perception.

One of the values of an internship is transforming "I think I might like this" into "I know what I like after actually experiencing it."

If you're already quite certain about your direction, your first internship can focus more on its transferable value for the next step: what new evidence will I gain after this experience that can help me apply for my next role?

When choosing, you might also ask yourself:

> Can it help me better understand this role I might like?

> Can it add new evidence for my next application?

If you can answer either of these questions, the experience usually already holds value.

### 2. Does your first internship absolutely need to be at a company with a big name?

The name of a well-known company can indeed help recruiters quickly understand an experience, so a Big Name or Big Title does have some value.

What's equally worth paying attention to in your first internship is: **actual work content + tangible outcomes.**

If Company A is very well-known, but the work mainly involves organizing documents; while Company B is smaller, but allows you to:

*   Participate in a real project;
*   Use common industry tools;
*   Collaborate with full-time employees;
*   And ultimately have achievements you can put on your resume.

Then Company B might provide more content you can elaborate on for your next application. Of course, if a well-known company's role also offers real responsibilities, reasonable guidance, and good projects, it would naturally be an excellent choice. There's no need to make the company logo and learning value an either/or decision here.

### 3. Do internship experiences need to always be "vertical"?

In the early stages of your career development, you can allow yourself room for exploration, especially when you haven't actually experienced different roles yet.

For example:

> Marketing Intern → Data Analyst Intern → Product Intern

Even with different job titles, there can still be a clear skill development path:

> Understanding users and the market → Learning to analyze problems with data → Bringing users and data into product decisions

Recruiters will typically be interested in why you made these choices and how these experiences led you to the role you're applying for now. "Vertical" here doesn't mean identical job titles; what's more important to consider is:

> Did the next experience bring me a bit closer to my target role?

Once you've actually experienced a direction and started to clarify "I'm willing to continue doing this type of work," you can gradually increase the proportion of relevant experiences. Each experience adds more evidence for your next application, and recruiters will also find it easier to understand your development path.
