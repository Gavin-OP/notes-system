# Finding and Screening Suitable Roles

After understanding the industry, the next step is to turn your direction into a set of real, actively hiring opportunities that are worth your effort. Job searching isn't about collecting as many links as possible from across the internet; it's about consistently surfacing information that's a good fit for you.

## Building Complementary Information Channels

- **Company Career Websites:** Verify roles, qualifications, locations, deadlines, and official application portals.
- **University Career Platforms:** Check for campus-exclusive roles, info sessions, and recruiting events.
- **Professional Platforms like LinkedIn:** Expand job titles, set up alerts, research teams, and past employees.
- **Industry Associations, Career Fairs, and Communities:** Discover niche areas and companies you might not be familiar with.
- **Experience Sharing Platforms:** Understand processes and candidate experiences, while cross-referencing years, regions, and roles.

After a role appears on an aggregate platform, double-checking it on the company's official website can reduce applications for expired, duplicate, or unverified listings.

## Expand Keywords, Don't Just Search for One Job Title

Combine searches based on job content, skills, and similar job titles. For example, if you want to work in data analysis, you can also look for titles like Product Analyst, BI Analyst, Operations Analyst, and Marketing Analyst, then use the specific responsibilities to determine if they are similar.

You can save your search criteria separately: job keywords, location, Graduate / Entry Level, industry, language, or work arrangement. Platform alerts will bring new roles to you, so you only need to screen them periodically instead of starting from scratch every time.

## When Reading a JD, Look for the 'Work' First, Then the 'Requirements'

First, circle the verbs and objects in the daily responsibilities: what you'll analyze, who you'll serve, who you'll collaborate with, and what you'll ultimately deliver. Then distinguish between:

- **Qualifications and Hard Requirements:** Degree, graduation window, legal or professional certifications, etc.
- **Core Competencies:** Skills used frequently on the job and key for screening.
- **Soft Skills and Work Style:** Leadership, communication, detail-oriented, ownership, etc.
- **Bonus Points:** Helpful to have, but you might still be a strong candidate even if you lack some of them.

The same job title can mean completely different things in different teams. Responsibilities are closer to the actual work than the title, and frequently mentioned requirements are more worth investing in than occasionally appearing buzzwords.

## When Screening, Consider Your Interest, Fit, and the Opportunity

| Dimension | Ask Yourself |
| --- | --- |
| Job Content | Am I willing to consistently perform these specific tasks? |
| Evidence Match | What experiences can I use to address the core requirements? |
| Learning Space | What skills will I gain from this job? |
| Growth Flexibility | What are the next steps in my path and the transferable skills I'll acquire? |
| Team & Environment | Do the work style, location, and culture align with me? |
| Preparation Cost | What will it take to get to the next round? Is it worth the investment now? |

You can still try for roles that are a bit outside your immediate comfort zone. The purpose of screening is to understand why you're applying and what to prepare, rather than waiting until every single item is a 100% match.

## Identify Information Worth Further Confirmation

Team placement, rotation arrangements, daily tasks, hiring timelines, and new hire support that aren't clear in the JD can be confirmed during info sessions, in emails from recruiters, or through Coffee Chats. Employee insights can offer additional perspectives on the work experience, but one person's team doesn't represent the entire company, so hearing from multiple perspectives will give you a more complete picture.

## Make Your Search Results Increasingly Relevant to You

Keep a record of which channel a role came from, whether you advanced to the next round, and what truly attracted or deterred you. Over time, you'll start to see which keywords are more accurate, which channels have more noise, and which roles, though applicable, don't truly motivate you. Your search system will become more streamlined as a result, rather than becoming increasingly cluttered.
